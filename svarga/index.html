PART VII · STRĪ & ŚĀNTI PARVASTHE WEEPING FIELD & THE BED OF ARROWS

A crown of ash
and a mother's curse.

What happens the morning after you win the world,
and find no one left to share it.

Of all the millions who came to Kurukṣetra, a handful walked away: the five Pāṇḍavas, Krishna and Sātyaki on one side; Aśvatthāman, Kṛpa and Kṛtavarman on the other; and Yuyutsu, the one son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra who had crossed over before the war.

Then the women came to the field: Gāndhārī, Kuntī, Draupadī, and the widows of both armies, by the thousand, searching the dead for their husbands and sons.

HASTINĀPURATHE IRON EMBRACE

The blind king
asked to embrace Bhīma.

The Pāṇḍavas came to bow before Dhṛtarāṣṭra. He embraced Yudhiṣṭhira coldly, then held out his arms for Bhīma, who had killed all his sons. Krishna understood. He drew Bhīma back and pushed forward an iron image of Bhīma, the one Duryodhana had practised against for thirteen years.

The old king crushed the iron to pieces against his chest and fell, blood at his mouth, weeping that he had killed Bhīma. Only then did Krishna tell him the truth, and reprove him gently; and the king's rage left him.

THE FIELD OF KURUKṢETRAGĀNDHĀRĪ'S CURSE

“You could have stopped it.”
He bowed,
and accepted.

Vyāsa gave Gāndhārī divine sight to see the field. Her anger was so great that a single glance beneath her blindfold scorched the toenail of Yudhiṣṭhira as he bowed before her. She saw her hundred sons, and Abhimanyu's young face, and turned to Krishna.

He could have stopped the war, she said, and had let it happen. By the power of her devotion she cursed him: in thirty-six years his own people, the Yādavas, would destroy one another, and he would die alone in a wilderness. Krishna smiled sadly. None but the Yādavas could destroy the Yādavas, he said; she had only spoken what was already coming.

THE BANK OF THE GAṄGĀTHE WATER OFFERING

“Offer water also
for Karṇa.
He was your eldest brother.”

At the river the brothers poured water for the dead. Kuntī broke down and told them: Karṇa, the charioteer's son they had hated all their lives, was her firstborn, born of the Sun before her marriage.

Yudhiṣṭhira remembered how, even in the dice hall, his anger had cooled whenever he looked at Karṇa's feet, so like their mother's. In his grief he declared that no woman would ever again be able to keep a secret, and resolved to give up the throne and go into the forest.

KURUKṢETRA · THE WINTERTHE LAST TEACHING

A king may not flee
into grief.
The weak would perish.

Krishna led Yudhiṣṭhira back to the bed of arrows, where Bhīṣma still lay, waiting for the sun to turn north. Through fifty-eight nights the grandsire taught him what a king owes: the duty of the ruler, the law in times of distress, the way to freedom. A king, he said, carries the burden so that the weak do not perish; he may not leave it to nurse his own sorrow.

When the sun turned north, Bhīṣma fixed his eyes on Krishna and released his life. It left through the crown of his head like a great meteor and rose into the sky. Yudhiṣṭhira took up the crown, and in time performed the horse sacrifice.

ĀŚRAMAVĀSIKA PARVATHE FOREST FIRE

The fire came through the trees.
They did not get up.

Fifteen years after the war, the blind king left Hastināpura for the forest, and Gāndhārī went with him. Kuntī went too: she had urged the war for her sons' sake, she said, not for any throne, and she would not stay to enjoy it. Vidura followed them, and in the forest he gave up his life in yoga, passing into Yudhiṣṭhira.

Two years later a forest fire swept toward their hermitage. Too old to flee, the three sat down facing east, fixed their minds in yoga, and let the fire take them. Sañjaya, at their word, escaped to the mountains. When Nārada brought the news to Hastināpura, Yudhiṣṭhira wept for his mother as he had not wept since the war.

PART VIII · MAUSALA PARVATHIRTY-SIX YEARS LATER

The last man
and the stray dog.

When empires fade and gods depart,
one last test waits at the roof of the world.

Thirty-six years after the war, Gāndhārī's words came true. Young Yādavas dressed Krishna's son Sāmba as a pregnant woman and asked visiting sages what she would bear. “An iron club,” the sages said, “that will destroy your race.” Sāmba bore it; they ground it to powder and threw it in the sea, and on that shore grew a tall, hard grass.

At Prabhāsa the Yādavas drank, quarrelled and fought; the grass they pulled became clubs of iron in their hands, until none was left alive. Balarāma sat in meditation by the sea and left his body. And when Arjuna came to lead the women and children away, the ocean rose and covered golden Dvārakā.

THE FORESTTHE HUNTER CALLED OLD AGE

A hunter named Jarā
saw what he thought
was a deer.

Krishna went into the forest and lay down, deep in yoga. A hunter called Jarā, which means old age, mistook him for a deer and shot him in the sole of the foot. (That the arrowhead was forged from the last iron shard, swallowed by a fish, is a later telling.)

The hunter ran up and fell at his feet in horror. Krishna comforted him, and rose, filling the sky with light, to his own abode. Arjuna, leading the survivors home, found his Gāṇḍīva too heavy to string and his weapons deaf to his call; herdsmen robbed the women in front of him. “Krishna has gone,” he told Yudhiṣṭhira, “and our strength with him.”

MAHĀPRASTHĀNIKA PARVATHE GREAT JOURNEY

One by one,
they fell.
He did not look back.

They crowned Parīkṣit, Arjuna's grandson, put on bark once more and walked north toward Mount Meru. A dog followed Yudhiṣṭhira from the gates of Hastināpura.

In the snow they fell. Draupadī first: she had loved Arjuna more than the rest. Sahadeva: he thought no one as wise as himself. Nakula: no one as beautiful. Arjuna: he had boasted he would burn the enemy in a day, and scorned other archers. Bhīma last: he ate too much and boasted of his strength. Each time Bhīma asked why, Yudhiṣṭhira told him, and kept walking.

THE ROOF OF THE WORLDTHE DOG

“Keep your heaven.
Not without him.”

Alone but for the dog, Yudhiṣṭhira saw Indra come down in his chariot to carry him to heaven in his body. “Leave the dog,” said Indra. “There is no place in heaven for those who keep dogs.”

Yudhiṣṭhira refused. To abandon one who is devoted to you, he said, is as great a sin as killing a brahmin. Then the dog was gone, and Dharma stood there, his father, testing him once more: at the lake he had chosen Mādrī's son out of fairness; now he had refused heaven for a dog. There was none like him in heaven or earth.

SVARGĀROHAṆA PARVATHE LAST ILLUSION

Heaven without his brothers
was no heaven.

In heaven he found Duryodhana seated in glory, and his brothers, Draupadī and Karṇa nowhere. Take me to them, he said. A messenger led him down a dark road of blood and fire, where voices cried out that his coming brought a cool breeze to their pain. They were his brothers, Karṇa, Draupadī. He sent the messenger away: he would stay.

The darkness vanished. It had been an illusion, the gods told him, shown to him for a moment because he had once deceived Droṇa. He bathed in the river of heaven, left his human body and his sorrow behind, and found them all: Krishna, his brothers, Draupadī, and Karṇa, his eldest brother, at peace.

THE STORY DIVIDES

A Bhīṣma's last lessons Seven teachings from the bed of arrows. Open each one. Open this path

From the bed of arrows,
to a grieving king.

Why must a king not run from the throne?

Without a king the strong devour the weak, as big fish eat the small. The king's burden is the protection of everyone else.

What is the king's first duty?

To protect his people; every other duty rests on that.

When the law cannot be kept?

In times of disaster even the rules bend so that life can be preserved; but what is done in distress must not become the rule.

What makes a person truly noble?

Conduct, not birth.

What is the highest dharma?

Not to harm: ahiṃsā.

How should a man meet loss?

Everything that is gathered is scattered, everything that rises falls; grieving over what cannot return only adds sorrow to sorrow.

Whom should one keep close?

Those who speak the truth even when it is unwelcome, and stay when fortune leaves.

Paraphrased from the Śānti and Anuśāsana Parvas in our own words.

B The night the dead returned Years after the war, Vyāsa called the fallen out of the Gaṅgā for a single night. Open this path

ĀŚRAMAVĀSIKA PARVATHE NIGHT THE DEAD RETURNED

For one night,
no one fought.

Fifteen years after the war, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Gāndhārī and Kuntī went to live in the forest, and Vidura with them. When the Pāṇḍavas visited, Vyāsa granted the grieving mothers a gift. At nightfall he stood in the Gaṅgā and called, and out of the water rose every warrior who had died at Kurukṣetra: Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Karṇa, Duryodhana and his brothers, Abhimanyu, Ghaṭotkaca, Draupadī's sons.

They came shining, without anger or envy, as friends. For one night fathers held sons, wives found husbands, and Kuntī sat with Karṇa beside his brothers. At dawn they went back into the river. Not long after, the three elders died calmly in a forest fire.
