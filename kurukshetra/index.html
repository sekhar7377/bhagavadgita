PART V · UDYOGA PARVATHE PEACE ENVOY & THE SONG OF GOD

Not even
the point of a needle.

A god's last offer of peace, a mother's confession on the riverbank,
and a chariot halted between two worlds.

The exile was over. Yudhiṣṭhira still shrank from war with his own family, and sent word: give back Indraprastha, or if that is too much, five villages, one for each brother, and let there be peace.

Duryodhana and Arjuna reached Dvārakā on the same morning to ask Krishna's help, and found him asleep. Duryodhana sat at his head; Arjuna stood at his feet. Krishna woke and saw Arjuna first. He offered a choice: his army, a million strong, or himself alone, bearing no weapon. Arjuna chose Krishna without a moment's doubt. Duryodhana left delighted with the army.

HASTINĀPURA · THE ASSEMBLYTHE LAST EMBASSY

“Not as much land
as a needle's point
will pierce.”

Krishna went to Hastināpura as the Pāṇḍavas' last envoy, and in the full assembly begged Dhṛtarāṣṭra to rein in his son before the Kuru race drowned in its own blood. Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Vidura and Gāndhārī herself pleaded the same.

Duryodhana rose and answered that without war he would not give the Pāṇḍavas as much land as could be pierced by the point of a needle. Then he made a plan with his brothers to seize the envoy.

HASTINĀPURA · THE ASSEMBLYTHE FORM THEY TRIED TO BIND

They tried to bind
a messenger.
The hall saw who he was.

Krishna laughed. Out of his body blazed gods and heroes and fire, flames from every pore; the kings shut their eyes. Only Droṇa, Bhīṣma, Vidura and a few sages could bear to look, for Krishna gave them sight. The blind king begged for it too, so that once in his life he might see this, and for that moment he saw.

Then the form withdrew, and Krishna walked out of the hall unharmed.

OUTSIDE HASTINĀPURATHE SECRET

“You are Kuntī's
firstborn son.”
“Then I am still
Duryodhana's friend.”

Leaving the city, Krishna took Karṇa up into his own chariot and told him the truth: he was Kuntī's son, born before her marriage, and so the eldest of the Pāṇḍavas. If he came over, the five would serve him, Draupadī would be his, and he would be king of the earth.

Karṇa answered that he knew it already. But Adhiratha and Rādhā had raised him; Duryodhana had honoured him for thirteen years. He could not betray them now. He asked only that Krishna keep the secret until the war was done.

THE GAṄGĀ · MORNINGA MOTHER AT THE RIVER

“You will still have
five sons.”

Kuntī went to the riverbank and waited in the sun's heat until Karṇa finished his prayers. She told him who she was. The Sun himself spoke from the sky and told him to obey her.

Karṇa did not. Where had she been, he asked, when he was set adrift, when the arena laughed at his birth? He would not desert his friend. But he gave her a gift: he would not kill Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, Nakula or Sahadeva, though he could. His war was with Arjuna alone. Whoever fell, she would still be the mother of five sons. She embraced him and went home.

HASTINĀPURA · THE PALACESAÑJAYA'S DIVINE SIGHT

The king could not see.
So he was told
everything.

Before the armies met, Vyāsa offered his blind son sight to watch the war. Dhṛtarāṣṭra refused: he could not bear to see his kin killed, but he wanted to hear it. So Vyāsa gave divine sight to the king's charioteer, Sañjaya: everything on the field, by day or night, even what men only thought, he would know.

The Bhagavad Gītā reaches us through Sañjaya's mouth, spoken in a palace far from the field, to a father waiting for news of his sons.

THE MOMENT BEFORE

Two armies stand ready.
Arjuna sees teachers, family, friends.
What once seemed certain no longer is.

The Bhagavad Gītā begins with this uncertainty. In the space before action, Arjuna turns to Krishna — and a dialogue unfolds about duty, the self, and how to live.

PART VI · THE EIGHTEEN DAYSDAYS 1–10 · BHĪṢMA COMMANDS

When every rule of war
was broken.

Day by day, commander by commander,
chivalry burns away on the plain of Kurukṣetra.

Before the first arrow, Yudhiṣṭhira took off his armour, laid down his weapons and walked across to the enemy lines. He touched the feet of Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Kṛpa and Śalya and asked their blessing to fight them. Moved, each blessed him with victory.

For ten days Bhīṣma fought like a fire in dry grass and the Pāṇḍava army melted before him. Twice, seeing Arjuna hold back out of love for his grandsire, Krishna leapt from the chariot and ran at Bhīṣma himself, breaking his own vow to bear no weapon. Bhīṣma lowered his bow and welcomed him. Arjuna ran after Krishna, caught him round the legs and dragged him back, promising to fight in earnest. (Vyāsa gives Krishna his whip on one day and his discus on the other; the chariot wheel is the image later tellers gave it.)

DAY 10 · SUNSETTHE BED OF ARROWS

He fell,
and never touched
the earth.

On the ninth night the Pāṇḍavas went to Bhīṣma's tent and asked him how he could be beaten. He told them: he would not fight one who had been born a woman. On the tenth day they set Śikhaṇḍin, who had been born a woman, Ambā reborn, before Arjuna's chariot. Bhīṣma would not raise his bow, and Arjuna's arrows covered him.

He fell before sunset and lay on the arrows, held above the ground. His head hung down; Arjuna fired three arrows into the earth for a pillow. He asked for water; Arjuna shot an arrow into the ground and a clear stream rose to his lips. There Bhīṣma lay, holding back his death until the sun turned north.

DAY 13 · DROṆA COMMANDSTHE WHEEL FORMATION

A boy of sixteen
knew how to break in.
No one had taught him
the way out.

Droṇa set the Kaurava army in the Cakravyūha, a wheel of rings that closed behind any who entered. Arjuna had been drawn away to the south by the Saṃśaptakas, who had sworn to fight him to the death. Only Abhimanyu, Arjuna's son, knew how to break in; he did not know how to come out. (That he learned it in his mother's womb is a later telling.)

“Break the gate,” the brothers told him, “and we will follow behind your wheels.” Abhimanyu broke it. But Jayadratha, armed with Śiva's boon to hold back every Pāṇḍava but Arjuna for one day, sealed the breach behind him.

DAY 13ABHIMANYU

When his bow was cut
and his sword broken,
he lifted a wheel.

Alone inside the wheel, Abhimanyu routed veterans and killed Duryodhana's son Lakṣmaṇa. Then six great warriors, Droṇa, Kṛpa, Karṇa, Aśvatthāman, Bṛhadbala and Kṛtavarman, came at him together. Karṇa cut his bow from behind. His horses fell, his charioteer, his chariot.

He fought on foot with sword and shield until they were cut away, and then raised a chariot wheel over his head like Krishna's discus. At last, as he rose from the ground, the son of Duḥśāsana struck him down with a mace. Every law of the warrior had been broken against a boy.

DAY 14 · BEFORE SUNSETARJUNA'S VOW

If Jayadratha lived
past sunset,
Arjuna would walk
into the fire.

That night Arjuna swore it before both armies. All the next day Jayadratha hid behind a wall of warriors while Arjuna cut through them, and the sun went down the sky. Then Krishna, by his yoga, cast a darkness over the sun. The Kauravas cheered; Jayadratha lifted his head to see the sunset.

The darkness lifted. “Now,” said Krishna, and told Arjuna what Jayadratha's father had sworn: whoever let his son's head fall to the earth would have his own head burst. Arjuna's arrows carried the head across the field into the lap of the old king at his evening prayer. He rose, it fell from his lap to the ground, and his own head split apart.

DAY 14 · BY TORCHLIGHTTHE NIGHT BATTLE

The spear saved for Arjuna
was spent in the dark.

The battle went on through the night by torchlight. Bhīma's son Ghaṭotkaca, born of the forest-woman Hiḍimbā, filled the dark with illusions and the Kaurava ranks broke.

Duryodhana begged Karṇa to stop him. Karṇa had only one weapon that could: Indra's Śakti, given for his armour and kept for Arjuna. He threw it. Ghaṭotkaca fell, swelling as he died to crush the enemy beneath him. Arjuna was safe; Krishna embraced him and danced on the chariot.

DAY 15THE HALF-TRUTH

The truest man alive
told half a truth.
His chariot came down
to the earth.

Droṇa could not be beaten while he held his bow. Krishna said he would lay it down if he heard his son was dead. Bhīma killed an elephant named Aśvatthāman and shouted that Aśvatthāman was dead. Droṇa would believe it only from Yudhiṣṭhira.

Yudhiṣṭhira said aloud, “Aśvatthāman is dead,” and under his breath, “the elephant.” His chariot, which had always run four fingers above the ground, sank to the earth. Droṇa laid down his weapons and sat in meditation, and Dhṛṣṭadyumna, born from the fire to kill him, cut off his head.

DAY 16 · KARṆA COMMANDSTHE VOW KEPT

Thirteen years later,
Bhīma kept his word.

Karṇa took command. One by one he defeated Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, Nakula and Sahadeva, and spared each, as he had promised Kuntī.

That afternoon Bhīma found Duḥśāsana. He threw him down before both armies and, as he had sworn in the dice hall, tasted his blood. In the retellings he carried it back to Draupadī, and she bound her hair at last.

DAY 17KARṆA AND ARJUNA

“Where was your dharma
in the dice hall?”

The duel of the age. Karṇa loosed the serpent-arrow at Arjuna's head; Krishna pressed the chariot down into the earth, and it struck away Arjuna's crown instead. Then Karṇa's curses came due together. A brahmin had cursed him that the earth would swallow his wheel; his teacher Paraśurāma, that his greatest weapon would leave him when he needed it most. His left wheel sank, and the mantra was gone.

He leapt down to lift it and cried out that dharma forbids striking a man on the ground. Krishna answered: where was dharma when Draupadī was dragged into the hall, when the house of lac was lit, when six men killed a boy? Arjuna loosed the Añjalika arrow, and Karṇa fell against his wheel. A light rose from his body and went into the sun.

DAY 18 · DVAIPĀYANA LAKETHE MACE DUEL

Below the waist,
against the rules.
A promise from the dice hall.

On the last day Yudhiṣṭhira killed Śalya, and Sahadeva killed Śakuni. Duryodhana, alone of a hundred brothers and eleven armies, fled and hid within the waters of a lake. Taunted out, he came up mace in hand. Yudhiṣṭhira rashly offered him the kingdom if he could beat any one of them. He chose Bhīma.

They were matched blow for blow until Krishna told Arjuna that Bhīma could not win by the rules. Arjuna struck his own thigh. Bhīma remembered his vow and shattered Duryodhana's thighs. Balarāma, who had taught them both the mace, raged at the foul blow and left.

THE NIGHT AFTER THE WARTHE SLEEPERS

He watched an owl
take the sleeping crows,
and knew what he would do.

Three Kauravas lived: Aśvatthāman, Kṛpa and Kṛtavarman. That night, under a banyan, Aśvatthāman watched an owl kill crows in their sleep. He went into the Pāṇḍava camp at midnight and killed the sleepers: Dhṛṣṭadyumna, Śikhaṇḍin, and Draupadī's five sons. The five Pāṇḍavas, sleeping elsewhere at Krishna's word, lived.

Hunted down at dawn, Aśvatthāman loosed the Brahmaśiras weapon and turned it on the womb of Uttarā, to end the line. Krishna saved the child, Parīkṣit. The jewel was taken from Aśvatthāman's brow, and Krishna cursed him to wander the earth for three thousand years, alone, diseased, unable to die.

A LATER LEGENDBARBARĪKA

Three arrows
would have ended the war.
He watched it instead.

This story is not in Vyāsa's Mahābhārata; it comes from the Skanda Purāṇa and from the temples of Rajasthan, where he is worshipped as Khatu Shyam. Barbarīka, grandson of Bhīma, came to Kurukṣetra with three arrows that could find every target, sworn always to fight for the losing side.

Krishna saw that such a vow would turn and turn until no one was left, and asked him for his head as a gift. Barbarīka gave it, asking only to see the war. His head was set on a hill above the field, and when it was over he said he had seen only one thing win: Krishna's discus, cutting through everyone.
