PART I · ĀDI PARVATHE TERRIBLE VOW & THE BLIND CROWN

The throne
nobody could keep.

How a son's supreme sacrifice planted
the seed of an empire's destruction.

On the mist-shrouded banks of the Gaṅgā, King Śantanu of the Kuru dynasty lost his heart twice. First to the river goddess Gaṅgā herself, who bore him a peerless son, Devavrata, trained in weaponry by Paraśurāma and statecraft by Bṛhaspati. Years after Gaṅgā vanished into the waters, the ageing king fell in love with Satyavatī, a fisherwoman whose scent was carried on the wind for miles.

Her father demanded a price Śantanu could not pay: Satyavatī's unborn sons must inherit the throne of Hastināpura, and Devavrata must never claim it. Seeing his father wither in silent grief, Devavrata rode alone to the fisherman's hut. Giving up a crown was easy for a warrior. But when the fisherman asked, “What if your children one day overthrow my grandsons?”, Devavrata stepped onto the wet stones of the river and spoke the oath that shook the three worlds.

THE GAṄGĀ · DAWNTHE TERRIBLE VOW

“Hear me, gods of earth and sky.
I renounce the throne,
and marriage, and sons, forever.
A guardian chained to the throne,
whoever sits upon it.”

The heavens thundered. Named Bhīṣma, he of the terrible vow, he was blessed by his father with icchā-mṛtyu: the power to choose the hour of his own death. Yet his noble oath became Hastināpura's curse. By binding himself to serve the throne rather than righteousness, Bhīṣma made sure that when evil one day wore the crown, its greatest protector would be forced to fight for it.

Satyavatī's sons died young and childless. Through the sage Vyāsa, the royal widows bore three sons who fractured the kingdom's fate before they ever drew a sword.

HASTINĀPURATHE BLIND CROWN

A king who could not see.
A queen who chose not to.
And in the shadows,
a brother with dice.

Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the eldest, was born strong as an elephant yet blind from birth: denied the crown by law, nursing a lifelong hunger in the dark behind his eyes. His bride Gāndhārī, princess of Gāndhāra, bound a cloth across her own eyes on their wedding day, vowing never to see a world her husband could not see, while her brother Śakuni stayed in the palace shadows, nursing a vendetta against the Kuru house.

Pāṇḍu, the second brother, pale and noble, was crowned king and expanded the empire across Bhāratavarṣa, until he shot a mating stag in the forest who was a sage in disguise. Cursed to die the instant he embraced a wife in passion, Pāṇḍu gave up his throne and withdrew into the Himalayan forests with his two queens, Kuntī and Mādrī.

Vidura, born to a palace handmaiden, carried the wisdom of Dharma itself, yet was barred from the throne by his birth: condemned to advise kings who refused to listen.

THE HIMALAYAN FORESTFIVE SONS OF THE GODS

One arrow. One curse.
One mantra,
and five sons
born of the gods.

In the mountain forests, Kuntī revealed a secret boon granted in her youth by the fiery sage Durvāsā: a mantra that could summon any god to bestow a child. At Pāṇḍu's request she called three. From Dharma came Yudhiṣṭhira, the steadfast voice of truth; from Vāyu, the wind, came Bhīma, of boundless strength; from Indra, king of heaven, came Arjuna, born to conquer the world. Sharing the mantra with Mādrī, the younger queen called the twin Aśvins, and bore Nakula and Sahadeva.

HASTINĀPURAA HUNDRED AND ONE

The first jar cracked open,
and the jackals
howled at noon.

Back in Hastināpura, Gāndhārī, desperate after a two-year pregnancy, struck her womb in grief when she heard Kuntī had given birth first. A mass of iron-hard flesh emerged, which Vyāsa divided into a hundred and one clay jars steeped in clarified butter. When the first jar cracked open as ill omens howled across the capital, Duryodhana was born, followed by Duḥśāsana, ninety-eight brothers and one sister, Duḥśalā.

Vidura begged Dhṛtarāṣṭra to abandon the firstborn to save the dynasty. Blinded by a father's love, Dhṛtarāṣṭra refused.

PART II · ĀDI PARVAFIRE, LACQUER & THE EYE OF THE FISH

Born from ash
and sacrificial fire.

A rigged arena, a palace engineered to burn,
and the impossible shot that won a queen.

When Pāṇḍu died in the forest and Mādrī followed him onto the pyre, the sages of the mountains brought Kuntī and five fatherless boys to the gates of Hastināpura. From their first day, the palace became a battlefield without swords.

At a river pavilion called Pramāṇakoṭi, Duryodhana poisoned Bhīma's food, bound him in creepers and rolled him into the Gaṅgā. Bhīma sank to the realm of the Nāgas. Their bites drew the poison out, and the serpent king Vāsuki, finding in him a kinsman's grandson, let him drink from eight bowls of their elixir: each one, the strength of a thousand elephants. He slept eight days on the riverbed and walked home stronger than before.

Then came Droṇa: a poor brahmin with a warrior's science, who had once fed his own child rice-flour stirred in water and called it milk. He taught both houses of cousins, and loved one pupil above all. Arjuna practised even in the dark, and Droṇa swore to make him the greatest archer on earth.

THE ROYAL ARENATHE STRANGER AT THE GATE

Everything Arjuna did,
the stranger did again.
Then he asked
for single combat.

On the day of the exhibition, Arjuna loosed the weapons of fire, water and wind from a single bow, and Hastināpura rose to hail him without equal. Then the arena gates thundered. In strode Karṇa, tall as a golden palm, wearing armour grown into his skin and earrings that flashed like the sun. He repeated every feat, and challenged Arjuna to fight.

Kṛpa, the royal preceptor, asked him to name his father and his line, since princes duel only their equals. Karṇa lowered his head, silent, like a lotus bent under rain. Duryodhana leapt down into the sand: “If birth is the measure, I make him a king.” Brahmins were called, a golden seat brought, and Karṇa was anointed king of Aṅga there and then.

THE ROYAL ARENATHE KING OF AṄGA

He laid a king's crown
at a charioteer's feet.

At that moment old Adhiratha hobbled into the arena, his shawl slipping, trembling with joy. Karṇa, still wet from the anointing, bowed his crowned head to the old man's dusty feet. Bhīma laughed: “Pick up a whip, charioteer's son. You are not fit to die by Arjuna's arrows.” Duryodhana answered for him, and took Karṇa's hand before the whole court. From that day Karṇa's life was Duryodhana's.

High in the stands, one woman had known Karṇa from the moment he walked in, by the armour and the earrings she had last seen on a newborn. Kuntī fainted, and was revived with sandal water. She said nothing. Neither son knew the other was his brother.

Soon Yudhiṣṭhira's goodness made Dhṛtarāṣṭra name him heir. Duryodhana, Duḥśāsana, Karṇa and Śakuni planned a polite execution: a festival at Vāraṇāvata, and a house for the guests built by the minister Purocana out of lac, resin, ghee and hemp, a palace made to burn.

VĀRAṆĀVATA · MIDNIGHTTHE HOUSE OF LAC

The palace burned above them.
They walked.

Vidura had warned Yudhiṣṭhira at the city gate in a hidden tongue, and sent him a miner who dug, night after night, a tunnel under the floor. For a year the brothers hunted by day and slept armed. On the chosen night Bhīma set the fire himself, first at Purocana's door, and the brothers carried their mother through the earth into the forest. On the bank of the Gaṅgā, a boat sent by Vidura waited in the moonlight.

In the ashes the townsfolk found Purocana, and the bodies of a forest woman and her five sons who had slept there after the feast. Hastināpura mourned the Pāṇḍavas as dead. Dhṛtarāṣṭra wept in public. Vidura, who knew, wept less.

Disguised as wandering brahmins, the brothers went to Pāñcāla. Its king, Drupada, had prayed for a son to kill Droṇa, and the sacrificial fire had given him a son and a daughter. Now that daughter, Draupadī, would choose a husband.

KĀMPILYATHE EYE OF THE FISH

He looked down,
and shot upward.

Drupada had set a great iron bow before the assembled kings, and high above it a turning machine with a narrow opening; beyond it hung the target. Five arrows had to pass through. Later poets made the target a golden fish that could be seen only in a pan of oil, and it is their picture we carve. King after king failed even to string the bow; some were thrown back by it.

Karṇa rose and bent the bow with ease. In the telling most people know, Draupadī's voice rang across the hall: “I will not choose a charioteer's son.” Karṇa looked at the sun, laughed bitterly, and set the bow down. The oldest manuscripts tell his turn differently; the wound is the same.

Then from among the brahmins a young man in deer-skin rose. He walked around the bow in reverence, strung it in the blink of an eye, took five arrows, and brought the target down. In the royal gallery, Krishna smiled and turned to Balarāma: he had recognised his cousins under the ash of their disguise. When the kings rose in fury, Bhīma uprooted a tree to hold them back.

THE POTTER'S HUT · DUSKSHARE IT EQUALLY

“Enjoy whatever
you have brought,
all of you,
in equal share.”

At dusk the brothers called from the door of the potter's hut: “Mother, see the alms we have brought today.” Kuntī answered without turning round. When she came out and saw the princess born of fire, her hand flew to her mouth. A mother's word could not be made false.

Vyāsa came to Drupada's court and told the reason. In an earlier life Draupadī had prayed to Śiva for a husband with every virtue, asking five times, and Śiva had granted it five times over. So the daughter of the fire married all five sons of Pāṇḍu.

THE FORESTEKALAVYA'S THUMB

He learned from a statue
of the teacher
who refused him.

Ekalavya, son of the Niṣāda chief Hiraṇyadhanus, came to Droṇa to learn the bow. Droṇa refused him: a forest-born boy could not sit among princes. Ekalavya bowed, went into the forest, shaped Droṇa from clay, and practised before the image with such faith that he became a master.

One day the princes' hunting dog found him and barked. Before it could close its mouth, Ekalavya filled it with seven arrows, without drawing blood. The princes stared. Arjuna, stung, went to his teacher: “You promised no pupil of yours would surpass me.”

Droṇa walked into the forest. “If you are my pupil,” he said, “give me my fee: your right thumb.” Ekalavya cut it off with a cheerful face and laid it at his teacher's feet. After that his arrows were never as swift. Arjuna's fever left him.

PĀÑCĀLABORN FROM THE ALTAR

The fire gave him
a son for vengeance,
and a daughter
for the age to come.

As boys in the hermitage of Bharadvāja, Droṇa and Drupada swore friendship, and Drupada promised to share his kingdom. Years later Droṇa came to him poor. The king laughed: friendship holds only between equals.

Droṇa asked his pupils for one fee: bring me Drupada. Arjuna bound the king and laid him at his teacher's feet. Droṇa kept half of Pāñcāla and returned the rest. “Now we are equals,” he said. “Now we can be friends.”

Drupada wandered the riverbanks until he found the priests Yāja and Upayāja, and lit a sacrifice for a son who would kill Droṇa. From the flames stepped Dhṛṣṭadyumna in armour, on a chariot, roaring. From the altar itself rose a dark young woman whose fragrance of blue lotus carried a mile. A voice from the sky said she would bring the ruin of kings. They named her Kṛṣṇā. The world would call her Draupadī.
