PART III · SABHĀ PARVATHE PALACE OF ILLUSIONS & THE GAME OF DICE

The dice
that broke the world.

From the jewelled floors of Indraprastha
to the darkest hour in Hastināpura's court.

Alive, wedded to Pāñcāla's princess and befriended by Krishna, the Pāṇḍavas could no longer be ignored. To prevent civil war, Bhīṣma and Dhṛtarāṣṭra divided the kingdom, and gave Yudhiṣṭhira the wilderness of Khāṇḍavaprastha.

Where others saw a wasteland, the brothers built a city: Indraprastha, with moats like the sea and walls white as clouds. Its heart would be a hall no mortal could have made.

THE KHĀṆḌAVA FORESTFIRE AND THE ARCHITECT

A forest given to fire,
and a builder spared
from the flames.

The fire god Agni, sick from a sacrifice of twelve years, came to Arjuna and Krishna begging to devour the Khāṇḍava forest, which Indra shielded with rain. Varuṇa armed them for it: to Arjuna the bow Gāṇḍīva, two quivers that never empty, and a chariot with the monkey on its banner; to Krishna the discus Sudarśana and the mace Kaumodakī. Together they held back the storms of heaven while the forest burned.

From the flames one being ran to them with joined hands: Maya, the architect of the demons. Arjuna spared him. In gratitude Maya built, over fourteen months, the Sabhā: a hall of crystal, gold and gemstone where floors looked like water and water looked like floor.

GIRIVRAJA · MAGADHATHE KING WHO HELD KINGS

Before the great sacrifice,
one king had to fall.

Yudhiṣṭhira wished to perform the Rājasūya, the sacrifice that makes an emperor. Krishna told him it could not be done while Jarāsandha of Magadha lived: he held eighty-six kings captive in his fortress, meaning to sacrifice them, and even Krishna's people had fled from his armies to the sea.

So Krishna, Bhīma and Arjuna walked into Girivraja dressed as young brahmins, and challenged the king to single combat. Jarāsandha chose Bhīma. They wrestled for thirteen days and nights without rest. On the fourteenth, as the king tired, Bhīma lifted him, whirled him above his head and broke him. The captive kings walked free, and every one of them came to Indraprastha with gifts.

INDRAPRASTHATHE HUNDRED AND FIRST INSULT

He had promised to forgive
a hundred insults.
Śiśupāla spoke
the hundred and first.

The four brothers conquered the four quarters, and every king of the earth came to Yudhiṣṭhira's Rājasūya with tribute. When Bhīṣma named Krishna the first to be honoured, Śiśupāla, king of Cedi, rose in fury and poured insult on Krishna, on Bhīṣma, on the whole assembly.

Krishna had once promised Śiśupāla's mother to pardon a hundred of her son's offences. When the count was full, he called his discus to mind, and it struck the head from Śiśupāla's shoulders. A light rose from the body and entered Krishna, while the kings watched.

THE PALACE OF ILLUSIONSTHE FALL INTO THE WATER

He fell into the water.
They laughed.

Duryodhana stayed on in the Sabhā, wandering its wonders. At a floor of crystal he lifted his robes, thinking it water. A little further, at a pool so still it looked like stone, he walked straight in and fell, soaked to the skin. He struck his forehead on a closed door of crystal, and stopped short before an open one.

Bhīma, Arjuna and the twins laughed aloud, and the servants laughed behind their hands. Yudhiṣṭhira had dry garments brought to him. Duryodhana put them on, and took the laughter home. (The line in which Draupadī mocks him as the blind man's son belongs to later retellings, not to Vyāsa.)

HASTINĀPURA · THE DICE HALLWHAT IS WON?

“What is won?
What is won?”
the blind king asked,
at every throw.

Duryodhana came home burning and swore to starve himself to death. Śakuni smiled: “Yudhiṣṭhira loves dice but does not know them, and a king cannot refuse a challenge. Let me throw for you, and I will win you his empire without a drop of blood.” Dhṛtarāṣṭra built a new hall of a thousand pillars, and against Vidura's warnings sent Vidura himself to bring his nephew.

  1. Pearls, gold, chariots, elephants, the treasury.
  2. Cattle, lands, the kingdom of Indraprastha.
  3. Nakula. Then Sahadeva.
  4. Arjuna. Then Bhīma.
  5. Yudhiṣṭhira himself.
  6. “There is still one jewel,” Śakuni said. “Your queen.” Draupadī.

Later tradition says Śakuni's dice were carved from his father's bones. Vyāsa says only that he threw with deceit, and won.

THE DICE HALLHER QUESTION

“Did he lose himself first,
or me?”

Duryodhana sent an usher to fetch her. Draupadī sent him back with a question for the hall. Then Duḥśāsana went himself, and dragged her by the hair, hair that had been washed in the waters of the Rājasūya, into the assembly of elders, though she wore a single garment and was in her days of seclusion.

She stood before them and asked: if the king had already lost himself, what right had a slave to stake a free woman? Bhīṣma answered that the way of dharma was too subtle for him. Only one Kaurava, the young Vikarṇa, rose to say she had not been won. Karṇa shouted him down, called a woman of five husbands no better than a courtesan, and told Duḥśāsana to strip the Pāṇḍavas and her. Duryodhana bared his thigh to her.

THE DICE HALLTHE ENDLESS CLOTH

The more he pulled,
the more there was.

The Pāṇḍavas threw down their upper garments. Duḥśāsana seized the end of Draupadī's. No husband, no elder, no warrior moved.

In the telling most people know, she let go of the cloth, raised both arms and called on Govinda, Krishna far away in Dvārakā. In Vyāsa's oldest manuscripts no name is spoken: the cloth simply did not end. Yard after yard of new silk, of every colour, rose in a mountain across the floor, until Duḥśāsana sank down among it, exhausted and ashamed, and the hall cried out in wonder.

THE DICE HALLTHE VOWS

A vow
for every wound.

Bhīma's voice shook the pillars. He swore that he would tear open Duḥśāsana's chest in battle and drink his blood, and that he would break with his mace the thigh Duryodhana had shown his queen. If he failed, let him never walk where his ancestors walk.

Draupadī stood in the mountain of silk with her hair loose. In the retellings she swears it will stay unbound until it is washed in Duḥśāsana's blood; in Vyāsa, she carries the memory of that hand in her hair all the way to the war. Then, outside, jackals howled at the sacrificial fire, asses brayed and birds shrieked, and the blind king grew afraid.

HASTINĀPURAVIDURA'S WARNING

One man spoke.
No one listened.

Vidura had begged Dhṛtarāṣṭra not to hold the game: it would split the family and end the line. When the stakes began, he rose again: the child who had howled like a jackal at his birth, he said, should be cut off to save the rest.

When Draupadī's question met silence, Vidura told the hall the tale of Prahlāda: a judge who sits in an assembly and will not answer a true question takes half the sin on himself. Still Bhīṣma and Droṇa kept their seats, bound for a lifetime to the throne they served; Bhīṣma could only say that dharma was too subtle to see. (The saying that they had eaten the king's bread and could not speak against it comes from later tradition.) It was the silence of good men that let the hall go dark.

HASTINĀPURATHE SECOND THROW

Everything returned.
Then the dice again.

Frightened by the omens, Dhṛtarāṣṭra offered Draupadī a boon. She asked only that Yudhiṣṭhira be free, so her son would not be called a slave's child. Given a second, she asked freedom for the other four, with their weapons. Offered a third, she refused: greed, she said, is not her way. The king gave them back their kingdom and sent them home.

They were barely on the road when Duryodhana won his father over again. A messenger caught them: one more game, one throw. The losers would live twelve years in the forest and a thirteenth unrecognised, or go back to exile. Yudhiṣṭhira could not refuse. Śakuni threw. The Pāṇḍavas put on bark garments and walked out of the city, with Kuntī weeping at the gate.
